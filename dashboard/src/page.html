Business problem

Orders are promised a delivery date and a price. How often does the business keep those promises, and what does missing them cost?

A retailer selling sports goods, clothing and electronics in five world markets. This project compares the plan (promised delivery days, the category's normal margin, a discount limit) with the actual result for every order line, explains the gaps, forecasts the next quarter and automates the monthly check.

Why this period: public datasets with promised dates, discounts and profit for every order line are rare, because companies keep their order data private. This is the most complete public one. The methods apply unchanged to any company's recent data.

    How to read this

    Orders and the late rate are very steady over almost three years, so even simple models forecast them within 1–2%. The useful message for planning: without a change in process, about 58% of lines will keep arriving later than promised next quarter.

    The forecast uses the full data, not the filters. Months from Oct 2017 are excluded because the data changes shape there (see Method).

    How the analysis was built

    1 · LoadPython reads the public CSV, keeps 24 needed columns and drops all customer personal data.
    2 · CleanA PostgreSQL view removes cancelled and suspected-fraud orders and adds slippage, on-time and tolerance flags.
    3 · CompareSQL compares promised vs actual days and actual vs expected margin, with tolerance limits.
    4 · Business caseSQL estimates the profit given away by discounts above a cap.
    5 · ForecastPython tests four models on held-back months and keeps the most accurate.
    6 · ReportAn Excel VBA macro checks each month's KPIs against targets; this page shows the same result.

    Definitions

    • Slippage = actual days − promised days. Above 0 is late.
    • On time = delivered within the promised days.
    • Tolerance = up to 1 day late is accepted; more than 1 day is a breach.
    • Margin = profit ÷ amount paid (sales − discount), summed, not averaged per line.
    • Expected margin = the category's median line margin; a line more than 10 points below it is a margin breach.
    • Cap saving = discount given above the cap rate × sales, assuming the same orders and costs.

    Targets used in the report

      Targets are set for this study; a real business would agree them with the operations and finance teams.

      Data checks

      • 180,519 raw order lines, 53 columns, no missing values in the fields used, no duplicate line ids.
      • Sales = unit price × quantity on every line (largest difference 0.00002), and amount paid = sales − discount (largest difference 0.01).
      • 7,754 cancelled or suspected-fraud lines removed; they never shipped.
      • Discount rates are stored as floats (0.100000001), so they are rounded before grouping into bands.
      • From Oct 2017 the data changes shape: about 1 line per order instead of 3, and the category mix drops from 24 to as few as 8. Those 8,193 lines are left out, so the main window is Jan 2015 to Sep 2017.

      Limitations

      • Delivery and margin patterns are almost identical across markets, which suggests the public dataset is partly simulated. The method transfers to real data; the specific numbers describe this dataset only.
      • The discount-cap saving is an upper bound: some customers might not buy without the discount.
      • Expected margin is a historical median, not a company budget.
